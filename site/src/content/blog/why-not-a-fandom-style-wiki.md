"Wiki" mostly means one specific shape now: open to anyone to edit, hosted on a platform that also
hosts thousands of other unrelated wikis, funded by ads across all of them. Fandom is the biggest
example, but it's a model, not just one company. Hunt Games deliberately isn't built that way, and
it's worth being explicit about what that trades away.

## Open editing vs. authored

A platform wiki's biggest strength is also its biggest liability: anyone can add or fix something,
which means coverage can grow fast, but quality depends entirely on who happens to be watching a
given page. Pages for obscure entries can sit wrong for years; popular ones can pick up vandalism
that outpaces moderation. It's a real, well-documented complaint about the model — search "is
Fandom accurate" and you'll find people specifically warning not to treat it as a verified source
without checking elsewhere.

Every Hunt Games site is authored and maintained by the network rather than open to public edits.
That means slower coverage — nothing new happens on a page until someone here writes it — but it
also means there's one place responsible for a page being right, not an unbounded edit history to
untangle. If something's wrong, [you tell us](/contact/) and it gets fixed directly, rather than
you fixing it yourself and hoping it sticks.

## One property per game vs. one property for everything

Platform wikis take on nearly any topic that clears a low bar, which is how you end up with
thousands of wikis of wildly uneven depth living under one roof. Hunt Games only takes on a game
after confirming there's real, measured search demand for coverage of it specifically — which
means the network stays smaller by design, and a listing here is closer to a bet that the coverage
was worth building than a default that anyone could have started.

## Ads that fund one thing, not a shared platform

Both models run ads — this network does too, and doesn't pretend otherwise. The difference is what
they're funding. A shared platform's ad revenue funds the whole platform, including wikis nobody's
maintained in years; a single-purpose site's ads fund the research and upkeep for the wikis
actually in front of you. That's not a claim about ad volume or intrusiveness one way or the other
— just about what the money is actually paying for. Full detail on what runs and what doesn't is in
the [privacy policy](/privacy/).

## What this doesn't fix

None of this makes the network immune to going stale — a page here can be wrong or outdated same as
anywhere, and the only real defense is the maintenance stage described in [how a game gets
catalogued](/blog/how-a-game-gets-catalogued/). The difference is who's responsible for noticing:
on a platform wiki, it's the community. Here, it's us — which is a smaller safety net, but a
clearer one to hold accountable.
